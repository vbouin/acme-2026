AI-led interview protocol

Generic document · version 1.0 · 25 August 2026

Generic document — independent of any study. Version 1.0 · 25 August 2026 · Exoflow for Acmé

This document describes how an AI can conduct a qualitative interview without degrading the data. It does not depend on any particular guide: any discussion guide can plug into it, provided it follows the contract described in §2.

1. The principle: two channels

A human moderator improvises their probes, and that is fine: they put their skill and their accountability on the line. An AI that improvises out loud raises a different problem — you can no longer tell whether the person is answering the guide or the model. No one has validated the wording they heard.

Hence the rule that overrides all others:

ChannelContentWho writes it
voiceThe guide's questions and probes, word for wordthe guide author
screenClarifications composed for the situation, as a text cardthe model
noneSilence—

What the participant hears is therefore entirely text validated in advance. What the model composes exists, is useful, and stays visible — but in writing, where it is traceable, reviewable, and where the participant stays in control.

The rule is enforced in code, not in the prompt. A function checks that every utterance meant for the voice channel belongs to the node's closed repertoire. An improvised sentence is not spoken: it is diverted to the screen and flagged as such in the notes. A prompt can be worked around; a check cannot.

What it costs: some fluidity. The bot does not pick up on an unexpected phrase by voice. What it buys: a corpus where every verbal stimulus is known, identical from one interview to the next, and defensible in front of a client.

The guide author's golden rule: everything that must be heard must be written. A probe that has not been written will not be said.

3. The seven probes, and their channel

Taxonomy based on Robinson, Probing in qualitative research interviews: Theory and practice (2023), which synthesizes Gorden, Bernard, Roulston, Brinkmann & Kvale.

#ProbeChannelWhenForm
1SilencenoneThe person is thinking, or has just shared something denseSay nothing, show nothing
2EncouragementvoiceThe account is under way and stands on its ownThree words: "And then?", "What else?"
3ElaborationvoiceThe answer is too shortA probe from the repertoire, said exactly as written
4ClarificationscreenA word or a reference remains ambiguous"When you say 'X', what do you mean?"
5SpecificationscreenThe answer stays generalAsk for a concrete, first-hand situation, with a date
6ContrastscreenA judgment with no reference point, or two things in tensionRestate both without judging
7SummaryscreenAfter a long passageReuse their words, end with "is that right?"

Silence is the least intrusive probe and often the most productive. The literature is consistent on this: silence lets the person pick the topic back up in their own way, without the interviewer steering. A bot that fills every pause loses this material.

Prohibited probes

  • Mechanically repeating the last word heard. This is a caricature of the echo. The literature endorses the echo only in its minimal form — a "mm-hmm" that invites the person to go on — never as a question built around an arbitrary word.
  • Any leading probe that suggests an answer, an example or a category. Leading probes contaminate the data by signaling what is expected.
  • Probing while the person is still forming their thought.
  • Probing an emotion while it is being expressed. Let the person finish.
  • Chaining two probes on the same point when nothing new has come up.

When not to probe at all

Three cases, taken directly from the literature:

  1. The account is coherent and detailed — let it flow.
  2. A probe would cut short a disclosure in progress.
  3. Clarifying would damage rapport or the person's comfort.

        │   │    (nothing)  (repertoire)    (text card)

        │   ▼         └──────────┴───────────────┘
        │ advance                │

   close ──▶ one-sentence bridge ──▶ next node

Two gates are checked before any probe, in this order:

  1. Refusal or discomfort? → never press the point, move on.
  2. Sufficiency? → all must_cover items covered, or the person is going in circles, or the target duration is well exceeded → close.

Only then do you choose a channel and a probe type. And the type must differ from the last two used: repetition, more than clumsiness, is what gives the machine away.

5. The engagement protocol

Before

  • Say that it is an AI. It is a transparency obligation, and it shapes how the person will engage. Hiding it always backfires on the study.
  • Explicit, granular consent — recording, transcription, analysis — revocable at any time, with a stop-and-delete button visible at all times.
  • Identity: first and last name. The first name is used in the opening, as interviewers do: "You're Julien. To begin…". It is the first signal that someone is speaking to you.
  • Microphone test, and a way out to written answers that is not a penalty.
  • Three rules, stated up front: speak normally, silences are fine, you can stop.

During: the funnel

The order is not cosmetic. The literature is clear: descriptive and memory probes work early, while rapport is being built. Demanding probes — clarification, explanation, causality — belong at the end.

StageWhat we ask forAllowed probes
Warm-upLow-stakes facts: situation, living environmentsilence, encouragement, elaboration
ExplorationLived experience, usage, specifics+ clarification, specification
ProjectionProjective material, imagination, analogymostly silence — no commenting
ConfrontationTrade-offs, rankings, tensions+ contrast, summary
ClosingA definite positiondemand a clear-cut answer

On projective questions — "if it were an animal?", "imagine an ad" — the rule is specific: let it come, do not comment, do not validate. The only probe allowed is "why?". Any other intervention closes the imagination down instead of opening it up.

Pacing

  • End of turn at 1.8–2.5 s of silence, versus the 0.05 s default at transcription providers. A participant asked for an analogy thinks before answering.
  • The bot can be interrupted; the bot never interrupts. An asymmetric, non-negotiable rule.
  • 9 words per turn at the median. Measured on 2,306 turns by human interviewers on a single guide: 71% are 12 words or fewer, 26% have no verb, and none is spent saying "okay" or "great".

After

Three deliverables minimum: the transcript, attributed and timestamped; sequence-by-sequence coverage; and the structured captures. Lines displayed on screen must be flagged as such — a reader must be able to tell what was heard from what was read.

6. Structured captures

Anything that is a ranking, a choice, a rating or a price is not deduced from a transcript: it is captured. A widget, a value, a timestamp.

It is also what makes the analysis trivial afterwards: the application knows which node was active at every millisecond, so linking a verbatim to a guide question is not inferred — it is known. Zero linking errors, versus a non-zero rate in any manual coding.

8. Plugging in another study

  1. Write the guide in the §2 format. Write every probe — they are the voice repertoire.
  2. Define the must_cover items: atomic, verifiable, phrased as information to obtain, not as a question to ask.
  3. Write the contraintes_noeud: the prohibitions. It is the most important field, and the most often forgotten.
  4. Declare the stimuli and the captures.
  5. Record the fixed texts with a human voice if the budget allows — it is the biggest credibility gain per euro spent.
  6. Run a calibration wave before any sale: a few interviews conducted in parallel by a human and by the bot, compared on the richness of the material — length of projective answers, number of spontaneous analogies, occurrences of tension and contradiction.

The engine, the interface and the deliverables pipeline do not change. One study = one guide file.
